How a mechanical watch keeps time, one wheel at a time.

A spring, a handful of gears and a tiny wheel swinging back and forth several times a second. No battery, no chip, and yet it can stay within a few seconds a day. This is an interactive atlas of eleven movements, from an 18th-century verge to a modern tourbillon. You can take each one apart, put it back together, and watch and hear it run.

Inside every mechanical watch

Five systems, one chain of energy

Every watch on this site, from 1780 to today, is the same story told differently. Energy goes in at the crown and comes out as the hands moving. In between it is released in tiny, perfectly equal steps.

1

Power

A coiled steel mainspring inside a toothed barrel. Winding the crown stores roughly two days of energy.

2

Transmission

The going train: four or five gear pairs that turn hours into seconds. In the classic 6497, 80/12 × 60/10 × 70/8 × 15 teeth gives exactly 18,000 beats an hour.

3

Distribution

The escapement: a pallet fork with two ruby stones lets the escape wheel advance half a tooth at a time, and gives the balance a small push each time.

4

Regulation

The balance wheel and its hairspring swing back and forth 2.5 to 5 times a second. It takes the same time for big and small swings, and that is what keeps the time.

5

Display

Under the dial, the motion works turn the minute hand's one turn an hour into the hour hand's two turns a day, at a 12:1 ratio.

What you can do here

Not a picture of a watch, a working one

Watch it assemble

Each movement opens as a tray of loose parts. Scroll, and they fly into place one at a time, with a short explanation of what each part does.

Turn it in your hands

Rotate, zoom to the jewels, explode the layers, see through the bridges, and flip it over to the dial. Click any part to see its teeth, speed and material.

Wind it, set it

Hold to wind and hear the ratchet click. Pull the crown and the balance stops, so you can set the hands. On automatics, the rotor swings as you turn the watch.

Slow time down

At one-fiftieth speed you can follow the pallet fork unlocking, the impulse, and the drop onto the next stone. You can also hear the tick split into three separate clicks.

Time it

A timegrapher like a watchmaker's bench tool shows rate, amplitude, beat error and drift in six positions. Turn the regulator to correct the rate, or watch a tourbillon average out its errors.

Compare eras

Verge and fusee, the pocket chronometer, the Swiss lever, the co-axial, the tourbillon, the wandering hour and Spring Drive: five centuries of answers to the same problem.

About the simulation. Nothing here is hand-animated. Wheel sizes, centre distances, speeds and how the teeth mesh are all computed from each movement's tooth counts. The balance and escapement run as a time-based simulation, and the ticks are synthesised from the escapement's own timing. The 6497 and 2824 trains use published tooth counts. The others are constructed to give the correct beat rate. Calibres marked "type" recreate a class of movement, not any brand's design.

An atlas of the mechanical watch

Each movement starts as a tray of loose parts. Scroll, and it assembles itself — wheel by wheel, jewel by jewel — until the balance starts to swing. Then it's yours: turn it over, wind it, pull the crown, slow time down until you can hear the escapement's three clicks, and put it on a timegrapher.

Geometry is generated from tooth counts and layouts in each movement file — the train ratios, beat rates and escapement timing are computed, not animated by hand. Calibres marked “type” are faithful recreations of a class of movement, not replicas of a brand’s design.
